Today’s lesson

Learning intentions

  • To be able to classify data as categorical or numerical.
  • To be able to further classify categorical data as nominal or ordinal.
  • To be able to further classify numerical data as discrete or continuous.
  • To be able to construct frequency and percentage frequency tables for categorical data.
  • To be able to construct bar charts and percentage bar charts from frequency tables.
  • To be able to interpret and describe frequency tables and bar charts.
  • To be able to answer statistical questions about a categorical variable.

Success criteria

  • I can identify types of data.
  • I can construct a frequency table for categorical data.
  • I can construct a bar chart from a frequency table.
  • I can construct a percentage segmented bar chart from a frequency table.
  • I can describe the distribution of a categorical variable.

Key definitions and formulas

Key definitions

Categorical variablenames a quality or group: nominal (names only) or ordinal (names and orders)
Numerical variablea quantity: discrete (counted, ‘How many?’) or continuous (measured, ‘How much?’)
Bar chartone bar per category, with gaps; height = frequency
Percentage segmented bar chartone 100% bar, a segment per category, with a legend
Mode (modal category)the category that occurs most often: the tallest bar or longest segment

Key formulas

Percentage frequencypercentage frequency = number of times a value occurstotal number of values × 100%
Number from a percentagenumber = percentage100 × total
Segmented barsegment ends at previous end + its %

    Types of variable

    Two types, each with two sub-types

    Categorical data put people into groups. Numerical data are quantities you can do arithmetic with.

    Categoricalnominal (names) · ordinal (names and orders)
    Numericaldiscrete (counted) · continuous (measured)
    Variable
    Categorical
    names a quality or group
    Numerical
    a quantity: counted or measured
    Nominal
    names only · e.g. eye colour
    Ordinal
    names and orders · e.g. fitness level
    Discrete
    ‘How many?’ counted · e.g. cars
    Continuous
    ‘How much?’ measured · e.g. weight
    Not sure? Two tests
    1 · Does an average make sense?
    average weight ✓ → numerical
    average house number ✗ → categorical
    2 · How was it recorded?
    weight in kg → numerical
    ‘underweight’, ‘normal’, ‘overweight’ → categorical

    Classify the following data as nominal, ordinal, discrete or continuous.
    1. aThe number of chocolate chips in each of 10 cookies is counted.
    2. bThe time taken for 20 students to complete a puzzle is recorded in seconds.
    3. cMember of a football club were asked to rate how they felt about the current coach, 1 = Very satisfied, 2 = Satisfied, 3 = Indifferent, 4 = Dissatisfied, 5 = Very dissatisfied.

      1. ✓Answers
        aDiscrete: the number of chips can only be a whole number.

        dNominal: the numbers are not quantities; they just name colours.
        eOrdinal: weight was recorded in three groups that can be ordered.

                    Describing the distribution of a categorical variable

                    The mode

                    The most frequent category: the tallest bar or longest segment.

                    A report includes

                    1 · the context and how many
                    2 · the modal category, with its %
                    3 · percentages for the others

                    ‘Majority’ only when the mode is more than 50%; otherwise ‘the most common’.

                                Two cases

                                Few values (discrete): one row per value, as for categorical data.
                                Many values, or continuous: group into intervals: a grouped frequency table.

                                Choosing the intervals

                                • every data value is in an interval
                                • intervals don’t overlap, and there are no gaps
                                • about 5 to 15 intervals
                                • easy widths: 10, 100, 1000 …
                                • start each interval on an exact value: 0–49, 50–99, 100–149
                                • 2–5 (whole marks) = 2, 3, 4 or 5 · 30.0–34.9 = 30.0 up to 34.9

                                          Shape: what to look for

                                          Shape

                                          Is the histogram single-peaked? Does it tail off evenly (symmetric) or mostly in one direction (skewed)?

                                          Symmetric

                                          tails off evenly on either side of the peak

                                          x̄ ≈ median

                                          Positively skewed

                                          long upper tail, to the right

                                          x̄ > median

                                          Negatively skewed

                                          long lower tail, to the left

                                          x̄ < median

                                          Bimodal

                                          two peaks: often two groups mixed

                                          The tail names the skew. Salaries and house prices: positively skewed. Age at death: negatively skewed.

                                          Describing a distribution: SOCS

                                          SOCS

                                          Describe numerical data in this order, one sentence each: Shape · Outliers · Centre · Spread.

                                          SShape

                                          symmetric · positively skewed · negatively skewed

                                          OOutliers

                                          present or not present: they go with the shape

                                          CCentre

                                          mean · median · mode
                                          median is unaffected by outliers

                                          SSpread

                                          range · IQR
                                          IQR is unaffected by outliers

                                          SShapeThe distribution is symmetric / positively skewed / negatively skewed.
                                          OOutliersThere are no outliers / is an outlier at … / are outliers at ….
                                          CCentreThe distribution is centred at value, the median / mean / mode.
                                          SSpreadThe range / IQR is value.
                                          Skewed, or outliers? Give the median for the centre and the IQR for the spread: outliers don’t affect them. From a histogram, centre and spread are only estimates.

                                                    Logs are just powers of 10

                                                    log10 x

                                                    The log of a number is the power of 10 that makes it: log10(1000) = 3, since 103 = 1000.

                                                    Log of a numberlog10 x = b  ⇔  10b = x
                                                    number0.010.111010010001 000 000
                                                    power of 1010−210−1100101102103106
                                                    log−2−101236

                                                    Properties

                                                    number greater than 1 → log greater than 0  ·  between 0 and 1 → log negative  ·  zero → log undefined
                                                    Each step of 1 on a log scale multiplies the number by 10.

                                                    Why use a log scale?

                                                    Data over several orders of magnitude

                                                    0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100, 1000, 10 000, 100 000, 1 000 000 plotted on an ordinary scale, then as their logs.

                                                    Ordinary scale: eight numbers bunch up at 0, one million sits alone

                                                    Log scale: evenly spread

                                                    A positively skewed histogram often becomes roughly symmetric on a log scale.

                                                            Key definitions and formulas

                                                            Key definitions

                                                            Median (M)the middle value of the ordered data
                                                            Mean (x̄)the sum of the values divided by n: the balance point
                                                            Rangelargest value minus smallest value
                                                            Quartiles (Q1, Q3)split the ordered data into four equal groups; Q1 = median of the lower half, Q3 = median of the upper half
                                                            IQRthe spread of the middle 50% of the data
                                                            Standard deviation (s)the spread of the data around the mean; on CAS it is sx

                                                            Median positionposition = n + 12
                                                            Median, n evenM = first middle + second middle2

                                                                    Mean or median?

                                                                    Choosing between the mean and the median

                                                                    The mean and the median are both measures of the centre of a distribution. If the distribution is:

                                                                    • symmetric and there are no outliers, either the mean or the median can be used to indicate the centre of the distribution
                                                                    • clearly skewed and/or there are outliers, it is more appropriate to use the median to indicate the centre of the distribution.

                                                                    Symmetric, no outliers

                                                                    mean ≈ median: either can be used

                                                                    Clearly skewed and/or outliers

                                                                    the tail drags the mean: use the median

                                                                    SOCS: the C line names the measure you chose (‘centred at …, the median’), and the S line uses its partner (IQR with the median).
                                                                    Key box: Cambridge General Mathematics Units 3&4, 1F p. 52 · Sketches: teacher illustration (dashed line = mean)

                                                                          Look at where the median sits in the box and which whisker is longer: the long side is the tail.

                                                                          Median near the middle; whiskers about equal.

                                                                          Positively skewed

                                                                          Median to the left; long right whisker (tail to the right).

                                                                          Negatively skewed

                                                                          Median to the right; long left whisker (tail to the left).

                                                                          With outliers

                                                                          Dots separated from the box and whiskers by a gap.

                                                                                  The normal distribution and the 68–95–99.7% rule

                                                                                  Normal distribution

                                                                                  A bell-shaped, symmetric model. The mean sits in the middle, and the standard deviation sets the width: approximately 68%, 95% and 99.7% of values lie within one, two and three standard deviations of the mean.

                                                                                  68–95–99.7% rulewithin 1, 2, 3 SDs of the mean: 68%, 95%, 99.7%
                                                                                  Tail percentages16% · 2.5% · 0.15% in each tail
                                                                                  Use the symmetry: 68% in the middle leaves 32% for the two tails, so 16% in each tail. 95% leaves 2.5% in each tail; 99.7% leaves 0.15%.

                                                                                  Standardised scores (z-scores)

                                                                                  Standardised score

                                                                                  A z-score counts how many standard deviations a value is from the mean. Subtract the mean, then divide by the standard deviation. Positive: above the mean · zero: at the mean · negative: below the mean.

                                                                                  Standardised scorez = x − x̄s
                                                                                  Check the sign: a value below the mean must give a negative z-score. IQ 115 is one SD above 100, so z = (115 − 100) ÷ 15 = 1.

                                                                                    Populations and samples

                                                                                    Population and sample

                                                                                    The population is every member of the group we want to know about. Measuring them all is usually too slow or costly, so we measure a sample: a subset of the population. The sample’s mean and standard deviation are used to estimate the population’s.

                                                                                    Populationevery member of the group being studied
                                                                                    Samplea subset of the population
                                                                                    Choose the sample at random so every member has the same chance of being picked; then the sample statistics give fair estimates of the population values.
