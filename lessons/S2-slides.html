Today’s lesson

Learning intentions

  • To be able to construct frequency tables for discrete numerical data.
  • To be able to construct frequency tables for grouped numerical data (discrete and continuous).
  • To be able to construct a histogram from a frequency table for numerical data.
  • To be able to construct a histogram from numerical data using a CAS calculator.
  • To be able to describe the distribution of numerical data according to its key characteristics of shape, centre, spread and outliers.
  • To be able to construct a dot plot for numerical data.
  • To be able to construct a stem plot for numerical data.
  • To be able to revise the concept of log10 x.
  • To be able to investigate the effect of the logarithmic scale on the features of a distribution.

Success criteria

  • I can construct a frequency table for discrete numerical data.
  • I can construct a grouped frequency table.
  • I can construct a histogram from a grouped frequency table.
  • I can describe the features of a distribution from a histogram.
  • I can construct a dot plot.
  • I can construct a stem plot.
  • I can use a CAS calculator to find logs.
  • I can interpret a histogram with a log scale.

          Frequency tables for numerical data

          Two cases

          Few values (discrete): one row for every value from the minimum to the maximum, as for categorical data.
          Many values, or continuous: group the data into intervals: a grouped frequency table.

          Percentage frequencypercentage frequency = number of values in the intervaltotal number of values × 100%

          Choosing the intervals

          • every data value is in an interval
          • intervals don’t overlap, and there are no gaps
          • about 5 to 15 intervals
          • easy widths: 10, 100, 1000 …
          • start each interval on an exact value: 0–49, 50–99, 100–149

          Reading an interval

          30–<40
          at least 30 but less than 40

          30.0–34.9
          30.0 up to 34.9

          Percentages may add to 99.9% or 100.1%: that’s rounding, and it’s fine.

                    Shape: what to look for

                    Shape

                    Is the histogram single-peaked? Does it tail off evenly (symmetric) or mostly in one direction (skewed)?

                    Symmetric

                    tails off evenly on either side of the peak

                    Positively skewed

                    long upper tail, to the right

                    Negatively skewed

                    long lower tail, to the left

                    Bimodal

                    two peaks: often two groups mixed

                    The tail names the skew. Salaries and house prices: positively skewed. Age at death: negatively skewed.
                    Cambridge General Mathematics Units 3&4, 1C p. 21 (shapes: teacher illustrations)
                    Learn · 1C

                    Shape: drag to skew it DRAG

                    1. 1Symmetric: tails off evenly; mean ≈ median
                    2. 2Positively skewed: long upper tail; the mean is pulled up past the median
                    3. 3Negatively skewed: long lower tail; the mean is pulled down below the median

                    Describing a distribution: SOCS

                    SOCS, like a sock

                    Describe numerical data in this order, one sentence each: Shape · Outliers · Centre · Spread.

                    SShape

                    symmetric · positively skewed · negatively skewed

                    OOutliers

                    present or not present: they go with the shape

                    CCentre

                    mean · median · mode
                    median is unaffected by outliers

                    SSpread

                    range · IQR
                    IQR is unaffected by outliers

                    SShapeThe distribution is symmetric / positively skewed / negatively skewed.
                    OOutliersThere are no outliers / is an outlier at … / are outliers at ….
                    CCentreThe distribution is centred at value, the median / mean / mode.
                    SSpreadThe range / IQR is value.
                    Skewed, or outliers? Give the median for the centre and the IQR for the spread: outliers don’t affect them. From a histogram, centre and spread are only estimates.
                    Cambridge General Mathematics Units 3&4, 1C pp. 21–23 (SOCS order: class convention; charts: teacher illustrations)

                      Two quick, by-hand displays for small data sets

                      A number line with each data value marked by a dot. Equal values stack. Best for small, discrete data sets.

                              Logs are just powers of 10

                              log10 x

                              The log of a number is the power of 10 that makes it: log10(1000) = 3, since 103 = 1000.

                              Log of a numberlog10 x = b  ⇔  10b = x
                              number0.010.111010010001 000 000
                              power of 1010−210−1100101102103106
                              log−2−101236

                              Properties

                              number greater than 1 → log greater than 0  ·  between 0 and 1 → log negative  ·  zero → log undefined
                              Each step of 1 on a log scale multiplies the number by 10.
                              Cambridge General Mathematics Units 3&4, 1E pp. 34–35

                              Why use a log scale?

                              Data over several orders of magnitude

                              0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100, 1000, 10 000, 100 000, 1 000 000 plotted on an ordinary scale, then as their logs.

                              Ordinary scale: eight numbers bunch up at 0, one million sits alone

                              Log scale: evenly spread

                              A positively skewed histogram often becomes roughly symmetric on a log scale.
